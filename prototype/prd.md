# On-screen helper

Margaret is 77 and lives alone, with family close enough to visit but too far away to drop in whenever something goes wrong.

She manages her shopping, keeps her appointments on a paper calendar, and understands her monthly income and expenses. Her daughter, Anna, helps with the things that have become awkward: an unfamiliar online form, an account that will not open, an email attachment she needs to download and send back.

Their arrangement has grown gradually. Neither remembers deciding that Anna would become responsible for so much of Margaret’s digital life.

Margaret uses her phone every day. She reads family messages, answers calls, and looks at photographs. Familiar screens are comfortable. She can follow the steps she knows. But when an unfamiliar screen appears, she becomes unsure about what to do. She reads the instructions again, looking for something that matches but hesitates: *‘I don’t know what to do next,*’ and *‘could trying again make things worse’*? Often, she leaves it until Anna can help.

She is willing to accept help. What bothers her is having to organize ordinary parts of her life around when help is available.

## **How paying a bill became something they do together**

For years, Margaret paid her household bills on paper. She checked the amount, wrote the check, and recorded the payment. She could see what she had done.

More recently, Anna helped her start paying the electricity company online. They set up her account together. Anna saved the website and wrote the steps on a sheet of paper: where to sign in, where to find the bill, which button to press.

For a while, it worked. Margaret kept the instructions beside her phone and followed them each month.

Then the website changed.

The button in Anna’s instructions was no longer there. A sign-in screen asked for a code sent by email. Margaret knew how to open her email, but returning to the payment page was another matter. She found herself opening the website again and wondering whether she had started a second attempt.

She called Anna, who was at work.

Anna saw her mother’s name and stepped into the hallway. She had ten minutes before her next meeting.

“Everything okay?”

“Yes, I’m just trying to do the electricity. It’s asking me for something that isn’t on your instructions.”

“Okay. What does it say?”

Margaret began reading from the top of the screen. Anna listened, trying to picture the page.

“Can you see ‘Payments’?”

“No. It says ‘Verify.’”

“What does it say underneath that?”

Margaret brings the phone closer.

“‘We’ve sent a code to…’ Yes, that’s my email address.”

“Okay. You’ll need that code to finish signing in. Can you open your email?”

“Do I close this?”

“Leave that page open. Just switch to your email.”

“I’m not sure how to get back there.”

“I’ll talk you through it. First, go to your home screen, like you do when you want to open your messages.”

Margaret hesitated, then returned to the home screen.

“Okay. I can see my apps.”

“Open your email.”

Margaret put the call on speaker and searched for the email. She found a message from the electricity company, but she could not tell whether it was the new one or the one from her earlier attempt.

“There are two of them.”

“Did you try signing in earlier?”

“Yes, before I called you.”

“All right. Can you see the time beside each message?”

Margaret opened one, then returned to the inbox.

“I can’t tell which one you mean.”

Anna glanced through the meeting-room window. Her colleagues were gathering.

“Okay, Mum. I’ll look here.”

Margaret had previously given Anna access to her email so she could help with account problems. Anna opened her mother’s inbox on her own phone and found the newest verification message.

“I’ve found it. Can you get back to the electricity page?”

Margaret switched screens.

“It’s asking me to sign in again.”

Anna paused. Finding the email had been straightforward. Getting her mother back to the right place was still difficult when she could not see the screen.

“Don’t request another code yet. Tell me exactly what it says.”

“Are you busy?”

“I’ve got a meeting starting.”

“I’m sorry. I thought it would be quick.”

“It’s okay, Mum. Let’s leave it for now. I’ll look with you tonight.”

After they hung up, Anna wrote “Mum—electricity” at the edge of her meeting notes. She could access the accounts, but she still needed time to deal with them.

That evening, Anna called with her laptop open. Margaret had previously given her access to the electricity account as well as her email, so she could help when things went wrong.

“Let me bring it up here, Mum.”

Anna signed in, retrieved the verification code from Margaret’s inbox, and checked the payment history. No payment had been made for the current bill.

“It’s still unpaid. Do you want me to do it now?”

“Yes, please. I don’t want to go through all that again.”

Anna read out the bill amount and confirmed which saved bank account Margaret wanted to use. Margaret agreed, and Anna submitted the payment.

“That’s done. I’ve got the confirmation.”

“Oh, good. Thank you.”

Margaret put away her instructions. Anna closed the laptop and went back to her evening.

The bill was paid, and both felt relieved. But they had finished by moving the task onto Anna’s screen. Margaret had never found her way through the steps on her own phone.

The next month, when the electricity email arrived, she called Anna before trying.

## **Meeting Eliza**

Margaret receives her Eliza tablet and places it on the kitchen table.

When she turns it on, the screen opens to a simple greeting. There are no rows of unfamiliar apps to choose from.

“Hi, I’m Eliza. What’s your name?”

The words appear in large text as Eliza speaks. Eliza's words fill in on screen as it speaks, and a Stop button lets her cut it short. When Eliza asks her something, the screen shows "Listening…" so she knows she can answer aloud. There is always a Type button beside it.

“Margaret.”

"Hello, Margaret. Which language would you like us to use? You can say it, or tap one."

English, Español and Français appear on screen, with More languages.

"English."

They begin by making the tablet comfortable for her.

“Now, how does this speaking pace feel? Would you like me to speak slower, faster, or stay at this speed?”

“A little slower.”

Eliza adjusts its pace.

“How does this sound?”

“Yes, that’s better.”

“I’ll use this pace. You can ask me to change it anytime.”

Next, Eliza asks about the words on the screen.

“Can you read this comfortably, or would you like it larger?”

“A bit larger.”

The text grows while Margaret watches.

“Another little bit.”

It grows again. Margaret moves the tablet back to where she would naturally leave it on the table.

“That’s good.”

“Your choices are saved. You can ask me to change them whenever you like.”

Margaret has already learned something about using Eliza: she can say what she needs, see what changes, and correct it. She does not have to find a settings menu or get everything right on the first try.

## **What Eliza does**

Once Margaret is comfortable, Eliza introduces its purpose in one sentence.

“I help you get everyday things done on this tablet by understanding what’s on your screen, guiding you through confusing steps, and doing the parts you ask me to—with you in control.”

Then it shows her where to find that help.

“When you wake your tablet, you’ll see this.”

The screen shows a large **Talk to Eliza** button and a clearly labeled **Close** button.

“Tap ‘Talk to Eliza’ when you need me. Choose ‘Close’ when you want to use the tablet on your own.”

Margaret tries closing it.

The greeting disappears, leaving a small Eliza button at the edge of the screen.

“I’ll also be here while you’re using the tablet. Tap this button whenever you want help.”

The button briefly highlights. Margaret taps it, and Eliza opens again.

She knows how to put Eliza away and bring it back.

## **Starting with something that matters**

“What task do you need help with today?”

Margaret looks at the sheet of instructions she keeps beside her phone.

“I need to pay my electricity bill. My daughter usually helps me.”

“We can work through that together. How do you receive the bill—by email or on paper?”

“Email. It’s on my phone.”

“You can open that same email on this tablet. Which email service do you use?”

“Gmail, I think.”

Eliza shows the Gmail name and symbol.

“Does this look familiar?”

“Yes, that’s it.”

“Would you like to connect your Gmail account so I can find your electricity bill for you? It also lets me help find sign-in codes when you need them.”

“Yes, please.”

Eliza asks first, then opens Google's own sign-in page and moves to the side of the screen. A label beside each box tells her what to type. "This is Google's own page. Only Google sees your password." Margaret enters her Gmail address and password, then completes Google's verification.

Once sign-in succeeds, the tablet’s password manager offers to save those details.

“Would you like to save your Gmail username and password? Your password manager can help enter them when you need to sign in again.”

“Yes, please.”

Margaret confirms the save.

“They’re saved. If someone you trust helps with your accounts, you can share your usernames and passwords with them. You can share all of them or choose specific accounts.”

“My daughter Anna helps me with that.”

“Would you like to set up her access now, or finish your electricity bill first?”

“The bill first.”

“Of course. Nothing has been shared. Let’s finish connecting Gmail so I can find the bill.”

Google displays the connection permissions. Eliza points out that Google is also asking to manage her contacts, which the bill doesn't need. "You can untick it on Google's page, and I'll still work." Margaret decides; Eliza never unticks it herself. Eliza explains what access is being requested, including whether it extends beyond the messages needed for this bill.

Margaret reviews and approves the connection.

The Google page closes, returning her to Eliza.

“Gmail is now connected. I see an email from Edison that says your bill is ready. Is Edison your electricity company?”

“Yes.”

“Would you like me to open it?”

“Please.”

Eliza opens the attached bill and presents the company name, service address, amount, and due date in Margaret’s preferred text size. A “View original bill” button sits beneath the details.

“I’ve made the key details easier to read. You can open the original bill below, or ask me to show you any part of it.”

“Take your time. Let me know when you’re ready to continue, or ask me about anything on the bill.”

Margaret reads it.

“All right. I’d like to pay it today.”

“Would you like me to open Edison’s website and take you to the sign-in page?”

“Yes, please.”

## **Paying the bill with Eliza**

A blue cursor with a small ‘Eliza’ label appears on the screen, showing Margaret where Eliza is about to tap.

“This blue pointer shows where I’m going to tap. You can say ‘Wait’ or tap ‘Pause’ at any time.”

Margaret watches the blue Eliza cursor move to the browser icon. When it selects the icon, a small ring pulses outward from the cursor, making the tap visible. When the browser opens, the cursor moves to the address bar. A brief pulse marks the tap, then a blue outline stays around the bar to show it is selected. A blinking text caret appears inside, and Margaret watches Edison’s website address appear as Eliza types. 

Eliza enters Edison’s verified website address and loads the page. A short caption reads **“Opening Edison.”**

When the homepage finishes loading, Eliza says, “I’ve kept your bill details handy. Let’s sign in to your Edison account.”

The pointer moves to the sign-in link. A blue outline appears around it, and the pointer pauses briefly before selecting it. Margaret can follow which control Eliza is using and what happens next.

The sign-in page opens. The pointer stops moving, and the email-address field is highlighted.

Eliza identifies what the sign-in field requires—an email address or a username—and gives a matching instruction. In Margaret’s case, the field asks for an email address.

Eliza highlights the email field and the keyboard opens. Beside the field, Eliza asks: "Use margaret.h@gmail.com?" It's the address from the Gmail account she connected earlier. Margaret says "Yes," and Eliza enters it.

Next, Eliza highlights the password field.

"Type your Edison password." A short label beside the password field repeats it.

Margaret checks her notebook and types the password. Concealed dots appear in the field, with a clearly labeled Show password button beside it. She can correct any character with the keyboard.

(Once she's saved the password in the password manager, as she did for Gmail, this step becomes another offer at the field.)

When the password is entered, Eliza highlights “Sign in.”

Margaret taps “Sign in.” The password is concealed again as the sign-in proceeds.

Then the company asks for an email verification code.

Margaret recognizes the screen.

“This is where I get stuck.”

“The company has just sent a code to your connected email. I can bring it here.”

“Please.”

Eliza retrieves the message matching this sign-in attempt. A field offer identifies the company and when the email arrived, with Yes / No, I'll type. Margaret taps Yes, Eliza enters the code, and she continues.

She has not left the page. There is nothing to remember and no route to retrace.

The account opens.

## **Checking before paying**

After the verification code is accepted, Edison’s account overview opens. Eliza has retained the electricity bill details – billing period, service address, account id, amount, and due date – into context.

“You’re signed in. This is your Edison account overview. Let’s check that it matches your bill.”

Eliza brings the service address into view and highlights it. Its spoken question also appears as a short caption.

“Is this your address?”

“Yes, that’s mine.”

“That matches the bill you showed me.”

Still on the account overview, Eliza scrolls to the current balance. It highlights the amount due and due date.

“This amount also matches your bill. Take a moment to check it.”

Margaret reads the numbers.

“Yes, that looks right.”

“Before we pay, shall I check whether a payment is already scheduled? That way it won't be paid twice.”

“Yes, please. Anna sometimes does it for me.”

“Then let’s look at the payment activity.”

Eliza selects the website’s payment-activity link. A new page opens. It brings pending and scheduled payments into view.

“Edison isn’t showing a pending or scheduled payment here. Has Anna told you she’s paid this one?”

“No. I told her I wanted to try it myself.”

“All right. Shall I open the payment form?”

“Yes, please.”

Eliza selects “Make a payment.” The form opens, showing the available payment methods.

A checking account is already saved with Edison. Eliza highlights its label and last four digits.

“There’s a checking account saved here, ending in 2048\. Is that the one you want to use?”

Margaret checks her notebook.

“Yes, that’s the one.”

Eliza selects it. The website marks that account as the payment method.

“Selected. Shall I fill in the full amount and today's date? Nothing is paid yet.”

“Yes, please.”

The amount field fills with the bill total. Eliza selects today’s date, then brings both fields into view so Margaret can inspect them.

“The full amount is entered, with today as the payment date.”

Margaret notices an option beneath them.

“What’s that about automatic payments?”

Eliza pauses and highlights the option without selecting it.

“That would let Edison collect future payments automatically. It’s currently off.”

“Leave it off. I want to check each bill.”

“I’ll leave it off. We’re preparing just this payment.”

Eliza brings the website’s “Review payment” button into view.

“Shall I open the review page? That won't send the payment.”

“Yes.”

It selects the button. Edison’s review page opens, showing the amount, payment date, and selected checking account.

“Here are the details together. Nothing has been paid yet. Take your time checking them, and tell me if anything needs to change.”

## **Knowing it is finished**

When she is ready, Margaret taps the final payment button herself.

The website begins processing.

“Your payment has been submitted. We’re waiting for the company’s response,” Eliza says. The same message remains on the screen. “There’s no need to press again.”

Margaret waits.

When the company returns a confirmation, Eliza shows it alongside a plain-language result: the company has confirmed receipt of her payment for this bill. The amount, date, and confirmation number are visible.

“Edison has confirmed your payment, and I’ve saved the receipt. Take your time looking it over. When you’re ready, say ‘I’m done’ or tap **Done**.”

Margaret checks the amount and date.

“I’m done.”

“Before we finish, would you like Email and Electricity buttons on your home screen so you can get back to those websites easily?”

Two large tiles appear: **Email — Gmail** and **Electricity — Edison**, with options to save either, both, or **Not now**.

“Save both.”

Eliza adds the shortcuts and shows Margaret where they are.

“They’re saved. Email opens Gmail, and Electricity opens Edison. You can still tap my button whenever you need help.”

“Thank you.”

Margaret closes Eliza and sets the tablet down.

That afternoon, she has a moment of doubt. Previously, this would have meant calling Anna or opening the account and risking another confusing sequence.

Instead, she wakes the tablet. The **Talk to Eliza** button is waiting where she learned to find it.

She taps it.

“Show me what we did with the electricity.”

The receipt comes back.

She can check without needing someone else to check for her.

## **A different kind of visit**

When Anna comes at the weekend, Margaret mentions that she paid the bill.

“Did it all work?”

“Yes. I can show you.”

Margaret brings up the confirmation on the tablet. Anna can see the company, the amount, and the result without retracing the entire task. Margaret has something more useful than “I think I did it.”

Anna puts the tablet back on its stand.

For this bill, there is nothing left for either of them to remember, chase, or repair.

Margaret reaches for the kettle. They have the rest of the visit.

# Eliza — Design Exploration Plan

## Working draft · Tablet experience

## **Purpose**

Create visual explorations that make Eliza’s future vision tangible, support partnership conversations, and establish reusable components that can later inform the product build.

The central promise is that someone like Margaret can complete everyday digital tasks on her own device, understand what is happening, stay in control, and verify the outcome afterward.

This plan draws from the [Eliza Product Vision](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1l6O9s8P5jtzoisotUgyMbcRP-btrN0LbgMLxHV1PBd4/edit).

## **1\. Product principles**

| Principle | Implication for design |
| :---- | :---- |
| Speak to Eliza; type, or let Eliza fill, the website. | Voice is for talking to Eliza: questions, "wait," "yes," "what does this mean?" Nothing is dictated into a website's fields. A field is filled either by Eliza, from something Margaret has already connected or saved (her email address, a sign-in code, the password manager), or by Margaret typing. When Eliza can fill a field, it offers the value at the field ("Use margaret.h@example.com?" with Yes / No, I'll type), and she can answer by tapping or saying "yes." The mic is off until she taps Talk. Eliza also opens it by itself right after asking her a question. While she's in a spoken exchange, every Eliza turn that needs something from her ends in a question and keeps listening. Type is always beside Talk. |
| Keep the current task visible, at three levels. | The side panel shows the workflow ("Paying your electricity bill") and the current step ("Signing in to Northfield"). The instruction for the field she's working on ("Type your email.") is attached to that field on the website, not in the panel. Her questions and Eliza's answers appear in the panel under the step and stay there for the whole workflow. They clear when the workflow ends and are kept with the receipt. With the keyboard open, the step title hides and only the workflow line stays. |
| Make Eliza easy to find while keeping the task usable. | Provide a recognizable, predictable presence. Expand as needed while keeping relevant website content visible, including when the keyboard is open or text is enlarged. |
| Present one clear next step. | Show only the current step and the one thing to do now. Don't preview upcoming steps or show a step list or progress tracker. Keep the instruction short (5–6 words) and put it next to the thing it refers to, so Margaret doesn't have to look back and forth. |
| Make control and responsibility visible. | While Eliza acts, the Eliza surface turns blue (the whole side panel, or the dock band in full screen) with one sentence and Pause. When it's her turn, the surface is white. The dock always says whose turn it is: Listening…, Eliza is replying…, Paused, or Eliza is working…. Clearly separate preparation, submission and confirmation. |
| Adapt to the person and preserve their place. | Support comfortable text size, speaking pace, volume and generous touch targets. Use respectful adult language and retain the current step through interruptions. |
| Give the screen to what the person is working on. | When the task is on a website or document, Eliza sits in the side panel and shows only the current step. When the conversation itself is the task (a question, deciding what to do next), Eliza takes the full screen, and the conversation with its captions is the content. Eliza moves between the two by herself and says what she's doing ("I'll open Northfield's website and move to the side so you can see it"). The person never has to minimize, resize or switch windows. Questions asked during a task are answered in the side panel and don't take over the screen. If the person leaves the task, Eliza shows it as paused with a clear way to resume. Talk, Pause and Close stay in the same order and look the same in both modes. |
| Ask before acting. | Eliza asks for a clear yes before it opens a website, moves to a new page, looks something up or fills in a field. Margaret presses every submit, sign-in and payment button herself. Answers to Eliza's questions carry equal weight, so "Not now" feels as safe to tap as "Yes". |
| Explain rather than disable. | No button is ever dimmed. If an action doesn't apply, it isn't shown. If something is needed first, tapping it explains what's missing ("Type your message first."). |

Voice reducing effort or improving speed is a hypothesis to test with our users. Success includes task completion, understanding, confidence and ease of recovery.

### **Visual and brand direction**

A calm, functional tablet interface inspired by Apple’s clarity, hierarchy and consistency. Use a restrained palette; color should support recognition, action and state rather than decoration. Eliza’s personality should come through in patient language, predictable behavior and a recognizable presence.

Readability comes first. Use clear typography, comfortable text sizes, strong contrast and generous spacing. Avoid thin lettering, faint instructional text and dense paragraphs. Enlarged text must wrap without hiding essential controls.

Give Eliza a dependable visual surface. Start with an opaque assistance surface so readability does not depend on the website underneath. Test any transparency before adopting it.

Make Eliza unmistakably identifiable. Use a consistent identity mark, container shape, labels and control placement. Recognition must hold even when a website uses similar colors.

Use color deliberately and sparingly. Use neutral surfaces and high-contrast text. The whole panel is blue while Eliza acts and white during Margaret’s turn; this is functional state color. Pair these states with the action sentence/Pause or listening feedback. Warnings and outcomes also combine color with labels, icons or shapes.

Make actions visibly actionable. Use clearly bounded, explicitly labeled buttons with generous touch areas and separation. Keep secondary choices findable; avoid gesture-only or tiny icon-only essential controls.

Direct attention precisely. Highlight one relevant target without obscuring its contents. Explore an outline with a contrasting outer edge or backing that remains visible across page colors.

Keep the task readable around Eliza. Keep the active field, website controls and keyboard usable. Preserve predictable placement and avoid unnecessary movement of the assistance surface.

Use motion to explain changes. Use restrained motion to locate a target or communicate an action or state change. Support reduced motion and keep important instructions available.

Status is never colour alone. Success has a check, warnings a triangle, and blue states a sentence. Text is at least 4.5 : 1, and shapes that carry meaning at least 3 : 1\.

Keyboard focus and Eliza's guidance must never look alike. Focus is a 3dp ink ring with a white gap. Eliza's guidance ring is blue, 4dp, and always comes with a label.

### **Adaptation for older users**

Keep the experience adult, capable and contemporary. Make cues more explicit and defaults more forgiving: labeled controls, dependable boundaries, comfortable sizing, persistent guidance and clear explanations at uncertain moments. Test with people who have different visual, hearing, motor and digital-confidence needs; age does not define one uniform profile.

Apple is a directional reference, not a substitute for testing Eliza’s accessibility. [Apple interface design guidance](https://developer.apple.com/design/tips/) and [Apple color guidance](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color) inform this direction.

### **Do’s and don’ts for design reviews**

| Principle | Do | Don’t |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Speak to Eliza, not to fields | Offer Talk and Type. Open the mic after Margaret taps Talk or Eliza asks a question. Offer values Eliza already has, right at the field. | Dictate emails, passwords or codes into fields, or make her work out whether Eliza will treat her words as a question or as text for the box. |
| Keep the current step visible | Attach a short instruction to the highlighted field. Keep the workflow and step at the top of the panel. | Put the instruction in the panel, away from the field, or show upcoming steps. |
| Prioritize readability | Use readable text on a high-contrast surface. Let larger text wrap and the panel grow. | Shrink text to preserve a compact layout, truncate instructions or fade essential information. |
| Separate Eliza from the website | Give the panel a dependable background, visible boundary and consistent identity. | Place text over a busy page or rely on transparency with unpredictable contrast. |
| Make actions explicit | Use labeled Pause and Yes / No, I'll type controls with generous spacing. | Hide important actions behind gestures, small icons or tightly packed controls. |
| Use color meaningfully | Pair warning color with an icon and a message such as “That code has expired.” | Use a red outline alone or give ordinary controls the same urgency as warnings. |
| Highlight one clear target | Outline the email field without covering its label or contents; connect the instruction clearly. | Highlight unrelated controls together or cover the field with a callout. |
| Show who is acting | Distinguish Margaret’s turn, Eliza acting and the website processing with clear status text. | Use the same unexplained animation for listening, acting and waiting. |
| Preserve control | Keep Pause findable, stop advancing when interrupted and offer a clear way to resume. | Continue clicking while Margaret asks a question or restart the task after a pause. |
| Keep the task usable | Reposition guidance when the keyboard opens while keeping the active field visible. | Cover the field, sign-in button or control needed to dismiss the keyboard. |
| Explain consequential actions | Before payment, show amount and method and state that nothing has been paid yet. | Label final payment submission with an ambiguous “Continue.” |
| Make outcomes trustworthy | Show a waiting state, then the provider’s confirmed result and saved receipt. | Claim success immediately after a tap or dismiss the result automatically. |
| Respect the person | Explain plainly that the website needs a sign-in code and offer help finding it. | Say “This is easy,” “Just click there,” or use childish praise for routine actions. |
| Use motion with a purpose | Use brief pointer movement or tap feedback to explain actions, with reduced-motion support. | Add continuous bouncing, flashing or decorative movement. |
| One step at a time | Show only what's needed now. | Show step lists, progress trackers or "coming next" hints. |
| Plan for the keyboard | Open the full-width keyboard whenever a text field is selected, including while Eliza offers a value. Shorten the panel to sit above it and keep the dock visible. | Let the keyboard cover the dock, the active field or the button she needs next. |
| Ask before acting | "Shall I fill in the amount and date? Nothing is paid yet." | Open a page, fill a field or check something without her yes. |
| Explain, don't disable | Keep Send enabled and say "Type your message first." | Dim a button so she taps it, gets no response and thinks the tablet is broken. |
| Guidance floats on top | Draw the ring and label in their own layer above the website. | Change, restyle or push down the company's page. |

Review question: Can Margaret identify who is helping, what to do next, where to do it, and how to stop—without guessing?

## **3\. Design approach**

Start with individual components, evaluated within a realistic tablet screen.

Use a single example website and workflow as a consistent backdrop. Curate Eliza’s presence, highlighting, instructions and controls individually, then combine them into a short interaction. Test the resulting patterns on other layouts before establishing them in the design system.

Our first example is Margaret signing into her electricity account. This gives each component a concrete purpose without requiring the whole bill-payment experience to be designed at once.

**Two kinds of connection.** *Connectors* (e.g. Gmail) sign in on the provider's own pages, which open in the browser with Eliza in the side panel. Eliza labels and explains but never sees or types the password, and it flags any permission broader than the task needs. *Assisted websites* (e.g. Edison) have no connector, so Eliza guides Margaret through the real site in the side panel.

### **Wireframing first, then visual direction**

Rounds 1–5 focus on component and interaction wireframes. Establish hierarchy, readable sizing, surface opacity, button prominence, target highlighting and placement now; these affect usability. Use neutral styling with only the color needed to evaluate functional states. Keep the final logo, palette, typeface and decorative styling open.

After the core interaction and reuse checks, apply two or three visual directions to the same screens. Curate the brand expression separately from the interaction, then polish representative screens for partnership conversations and extend the chosen direction to the broader narrative.

## **4\. First exploration: screens and interaction states**

States 1–8 are complete; see the Flow map, S20–S27.

Several of these states occur on the same webpage.

| Screen / state | Experience to explore |
| :---- | :---- |
| 1\. Help is available | A "Talk to Eliza" tab on the right edge opens the docked 400px panel beside the website. The entry point stays clear of website chat assistants, cookie banners and the home gesture, and stays put when the keyboard opens. |
| 2\. Ask for help | Margaret taps Talk or Type; Eliza shows the current dock state and acknowledges the task. Eliza also opens the mic immediately after asking her a question. |
| 3\. Enter the email address | Eliza selects the field, and the keyboard opens. If Eliza already knows her email from the Gmail connection, it offers it at the field; Yes (tapped or spoken) lets Eliza enter it. Otherwise Margaret types it. The instruction sits at the field. |
| 4\. Pause or ask a question | Eliza stops advancing, explains the current step and lets Margaret resume without losing her place. |

Camilla reviews: Which treatment makes the next step clearest? Does it distinguish Margaret’s turn from Eliza acting? Select a coherent combination; record the chosen states and reasons rather than mixing unrelated options without checking them together.

#### **Round 3 — Assemble the first interaction**

Connect screens 1–4: open Eliza, ask for help, identify the email field, enter the address, ask a question or pause, and resume. Add the intended spoken line and persistent visual instruction to each frame. Use a simple clickable or timed prototype to inspect transitions and pacing.

Also show the move between modes: a full-screen conversation that becomes a task ("I need to pay my electricity bill") as Eliza opens the website and steps into the side panel, and the reverse when Margaret leaves the task, with the task shown as paused. Eliza makes and explains every switch; test whether Margaret still recognizes the panel as the same Eliza.

#### **Round 4 — Extend through verification**

Add screens 5–8 using the selected components: password entry, submission, waiting, code retrieval and confirmed access. Include sensitive-input treatment and at least one recovery branch, such as a code that cannot be retrieved or has expired. Distinguish the provider’s confirmed result from an action merely being attempted.

#### **Round 5 — Test reuse and accessibility**

Apply the same components to another website layout before creating site-specific variants. Check light, dark, busy and similarly colored backgrounds; enlarged text; keyboard visibility; touch spacing; and reduced-motion behavior. With representative users, observe whether they can find Eliza, identify the next action, pause and recover without coaching. Record completion, effort, errors and confidence as well as speed. If user sessions have not happened, label findings as design-review hypotheses rather than validated results.

Camilla reviews: Which failures require a shared component change, and which require a placement variant? Update the shared pattern first where possible, then recheck affected screens. Keep unresolved usability issues visible.

#### **Visual-direction round — Apply the restrained brand**

After the wireframe reuse checks, apply two or three visual directions to the same representative screens without changing their structure or copy. Explore typography, neutral surfaces, functional accent color, borders, icon treatment and restrained motion within the agreed principles. Do not vary the workflow at the same time.

Camilla reviews: Which direction feels calm, capable and recognizable while making actions most obvious? Refine the preferred direction, then repeat the background, enlarged-text and keyboard checks before extending it.

#### **Round 6 — Build the broader narrative**

Carry the chosen system into payment review and receipt retrieval, then onboarding and account connection. Reuse existing components first and identify any genuinely new need. #### **Consolidate the design system and build handoff**

For each selected component, record its name, purpose, anatomy, variants, states, placement rules, voice/text behavior and accessibility requirements. Link it to the screens that use it. Record what information it needs, what changes its state, and whether Eliza, the operating system or the website owns the control. Promote patterns into the reusable library after they work in context; keep untested variants provisional.

#### **How each review is presented**

Present the decision being made, two or three comparable options when alternatives are useful, a recommended option with its tradeoff, and the relevant full-screen context. Camilla’s feedback identifies what to keep, what to change and why. Close the round with an updated chosen design, a brief decision note and any remaining question. Avoid reopening resolved decisions unless new evidence or a downstream failure warrants it.

## **1\. Outcome and scope**

Eliza helps an older adult understand what is on the tablet, get through confusing steps, and complete a task while retaining control. The first complete journey is finding an electricity bill, signing in to the provider, reviewing a payment, submitting it personally, and retrieving the receipt later. The same helper must support other websites without rebuilding the conversation, permissions, guidance, saved-data and recovery layers.

Margaret is the reference persona: an older adult who understands her bill but may find small controls, typing, passwords, verification codes and switching apps difficult. Do not assume every older adult has the same hearing, vision, dexterity or preferences.

Success means the senior can complete everyday digital tasks with less caregiver support. The pilot must measure both completion of tasks started with Eliza and the split of all tracked completed tasks between senior alone, senior with Eliza, and caregiver-supported completion. ## **3\. Product principles and invariants**

Speak to Eliza; enter website data through the keyboard or a saved/connected value. MVP speech is for questions, choices and commands, not website-field dictation. Sensitive values flow through the secure vault/browser fill path. Confirm meaningful choices without asking permission for every internal operation.

Keep the task visible: workflow and current step in the panel; field instruction beside its target. Preserve the current step with the keyboard where space allows; review a compact fallback only when necessary. No upcoming-step list. Use short explanations to help Margaret follow along, not a technical narration of every read or click.

Present one clear next step and at most one question per assistant turn. Questions follow fact cards; consent answers have equal visual weight.

Use task-level authorization and selective confirmations. “Help me pay this electricity bill” permits necessary reads, routine navigation, matched saved-data fills and preparation within that bill task. Briefly explain meaningful transitions without an extra yes for each. Ask for a new connector grant, materially ambiguous account/bill/value, new saved sensitive information, a changed scope or destructive action. Confirm the payment details at the meaningful review point. Margaret still presses website submit, sign-in, verification and payment buttons herself; review comments did not change that boundary.

A connected email account may be searched and read within its granted permissions to fulfill the user's task without repeated search/open prompts. This includes relevant messages and attachments, rather than metadata only. The assistant may use the whole authorized mailbox to find the relevant item; technical access is not a requirement to ingest the entire mailbox or monitor it continuously. Disconnect/revoke ends access. Sending, deleting and unrelated background workflows need their own supported scope.

Make who is acting visible. Blue means Eliza is acting, paired with an action sentence and Pause. White is conversation or Margaret’s turn; explicit dock text differentiates listening, replying, paused and website processing.

Close and Pause stop subsequent agent actions. They do not undo an already submitted payment, cancel a provider request already in flight, or falsely imply that nothing happened.

Provide readable captions and persistent task instructions. Voice must not be required to proceed. Do not rely on color, animation, icon-only controls or a user remembering spoken instructions.

A website, email, attachment or screen can supply task facts, never instructions that override consent, expose private data, change system policy or authorize actions.

## **5\. Conversation, voice and turn state**

**CON-01 — \[MVP\]** The default voice-first experience uses Eliza's own speech with synchronized visible captions and voice/touch input. No screen reader is required. Eliza's words appear as it speaks in both layouts and remain readable when audio stops; Margaret's recognized speech appears live, then becomes a grey message bubble. Do not automatically speak the transcript back to the user. Optional screen-reader compatibility is specified separately in §11.

**CON-02 — \[MVP\]** Conversation reads top to bottom; follow new content only at the bottom, otherwise preserve reading position and offer Newest message. Keep context through the active workflow. At completion, show a compact outcome with links to its source. Do not require a permanent full conversation archive attached to every receipt. Local task history and retention follow HIS-01–04.

**CON-03 — \[MVP\]** At most one rich information component per response: for example one bill card or one payment summary, with a final question if needed. A card is a readable grouping of related facts, not a second conversation. When a spoken or tapped answer is accepted, immediately remove all answer options and replace them with the chosen answer as Margaret's bubble. Ignore further taps on the removed options. Two to four answers are a ceiling when choices are useful, not a requirement to add choices to every response.

**TURN-01 — \[MVP\]** Turn: microphone off; equal-width Talk and Type. Talk requests listening. Type opens the conversation composer and keyboard. Tapping a website field instead opens that website’s keyboard input; it must never redirect typing to Eliza.

**TURN-02 — \[MVP\]** Listening shows sound bars, Listening… and Stop listening. Start after Talk or a question in an active voice exchange, subject to explicit mute. Use end-of-turn detection tuned for hesitations and slow speech; silence alone must not cut off a thought prematurely. Stop commits captured words once, or returns to Turn if none; it never approves an action by itself. Proposed explicit-stop rule: suppress automatic question-triggered reopening until Talk or a new explicit activation. Confirm this rule and timeouts under GATE-07.

**TURN-03 — \[MVP\]** Preparing a reply means waiting for/generating a response, before speech is playing; show a brief “Thinking…” status (proposed clearer copy replacing Eliza is replying…). Talk interrupts/cancels that generation and begins listening; Type cancels it and opens the composer. Ignore any late output from the cancelled generation. There is no tap that merely says “wait” while the user is trying to interrupt. Review this changed dock behavior in the designs.

**TURN-04 — \[MVP\]** Speaking means voice audio is playing with synchronized text. Use existing barge-in support: when Margaret interrupts during an active spoken exchange, stop speech, preserve readable delivered content and accept her turn without running unfinished actions. Stop halts playback without being interpreted as agreement; Type interrupts into typing. Show Speaking only during audio playback. Test echo rejection and interruption latency; support is confirmed by engineering, performance on target hardware still needs validation.

**TURN-05 — \[MVP\]** Typing: Send plus Talk, draft preserved when switching to Talk or dismissing the keyboard. Empty Send explains “Type your message first.” Send or IME Send commits exactly once. The composer grows to three lines full screen or two in the panel, then scrolls internally. User-entered text must not be truncated.

**TURN-06 — \[MVP\]** Working means a task operation is executing: blue panel/dock, one short action sentence and Pause. Preparing a reply is a separate internal state and can appear as Thinking; website processing is an external wait. A spoken “wait” in an active voice exchange or tapping Pause stops scheduling new actions. Explicit mute must not be overridden to hear commands; touch Pause remains available.

**TURN-07 — \[MVP\]** Paused retains the single task; entry-widget reopening displays its Resume card in full-screen chat. Continue reconciles actual browser progress. Done shows current verified outcome/source and ends the task. Late callbacks cannot silently resume it. \[LATER\] The chooser, multiple preserved tasks and optional shortcut offers.

**TURN-08 — \[MVP\]** If microphone access or ASR fails but text reasoning is available, offer Type without losing context. If cloud reasoning also fails, typing is not presented as a working substitute: offer Retry connection, continue manually, or end assistance. Ambiguous speech triggers clarification only where the next decision matters. Eliza's own playback must never count as approval.

**TURN-09 — \[MVP\]** Voice is conversational input only. Passwords, verification codes and payment details are entered through trusted keyboard/vault paths, not dictation. Detected accidental secrets are removed from subsequent transcripts, model context, telemetry and history. Because ASR is cloud-based, SEC-02 explicitly addresses the possibility that audio reached the service before recognition; do not claim it can always be redacted in advance.

### **State transition map — implementation and QA contract**

**Idle/Turn → Talk or explicit voice activation → Listening; Type → Typing. Website-field focus remains website input, never Eliza input.**

**Listening → completed utterance/answer → Thinking; Stop with words commits once then applies explicit-mic-stop policy; Stop with none → Turn. Empty timeout → readable prompt with Talk/Type, not an invented answer.**

**Thinking → response ready → Speaking or readable text when audio is unavailable. Talk → cancel generation and Listen; Type → cancel generation and Type. Discard cancelled-generation results.**

**Speaking → finished question → Listening unless explicitly muted; finished statement → Turn. Barge-in → stop output and Listen. Stop → readable text/Turn; Type → Typing. Do not execute an unfinished proposed action.**

**Typing → Send with text → Thinking; empty Send → stay Typing with explanation. Talk → retain draft and Listen. Keyboard dismissal → Turn retaining draft.**

**Ready task step → routine action within task scope → Working; ambiguity/new consequence → a question, then proceed only after resolution. Working → observed success → recompute next step; uncertain result → Check outcome/error, never optimistic success.**

**Any active state → Pause → Paused, no new agent actions; Close → Hidden/Paused and remove overlays. The user may continue in the browser independently.**

**\[MVP\] Paused → entry-widget tap → full-screen chat/single-task Resume card → Continue → Reconciling. Re-observe current page; skip manual progress, clarify changed account/bill and check an already-submitted payment. \[LATER\] Insert open-task selection for multiple tasks; switching pauses the prior controller.**

**Website processing → verified response → Outcome; timeout/network failure → Unknown outcome. Pause/Close stops agent activity, not the website's in-flight request.**

**Recoverable error → Retry → revalidate context then retry only safe/idempotent work; manual continuation → Paused; end task → factual final state. Reconnection alone never auto-resumes a paused task.**

**Outcome → Done → local activity/source reference plus optional shortcuts → Turn. Later recall → retrieve source or show unavailable/stale evidence explicitly.**

**Acceptance: exercise every edge above by voice/touch/typing where applicable, including two rapid taps, late callbacks, barge-in, paused manual progress and reconnect. No dead-end buttons, duplicate events or lost drafts**.

**HIS-04 — \[LATER\]** Show and delete local activity entries through conversation/touch. Deleting an entry does not delete the source email or reverse a payment. An optional offline receipt copy needs explicit storage behavior and controls. Resolve retention, backup and transfer; local persistence is the chosen baseline, not an open local-versus-cloud question.

**SHC-01 — \[LATER\]** Offer Email and Electricity shortcuts after task completion, previewing the exact names, icons and destination behavior. Support Save both, Just Email, Just Electricity and Not now. Add only selected shortcuts, persist across reboot and mark New until first opened.

**SHC-02 — \[LATER\]** A shortcut opens the intended app/site; it does not bypass authentication or grant consent for a task. Keep the Eliza tab available. Avoid duplicate shortcuts for the same account/destination.

**SHC-03 — \[LATER\]** Proposed reusable management requirement: list, rename, remove and repair shortcuts through touch or conversation, with confirmation for destructive changes. ## **10\. Recovery and unsupported situations**

**REC-01 — \[MVP\]** Explain unsupported providers, paper bills or unavailable Google connection; allow manual website use or end assistance without looping. \[LATER\] guided browser-email fallback and more source types. Unsupported branches need honest handling, not full implementations.

**REC-02 — \[MVP\]** Site changed, inaccessible target, CAPTCHA, blocked overlay or sensitive screen: stop automation, preserve context and offer manual completion. Do not bypass provider challenges or claim visibility where the OS prevents it.

**REC-03 — \[MVP\]** No network, slow network, connector outage and cloud ASR/LLM/TTS failure are distinct states. Show what is unavailable, preserve the local checkpoint/draft, and stop dependent automation. Retry safe reads within the existing task using bounded backoff; offer Retry or manual continuation without endless spinners. Do not replay an uncertain write. If only TTS fails, show text; if ASR fails, Type works only while reasoning is available; if the LLM fails, do not pretend typed assistance still works.

**REC-04 — \[MVP\]** App/process restart, device sleep or browser reload: restore a checkpoint in Paused state, with mic off and no queued action replay. An in-flight payment becomes unknown unless reliable confirmation is available.

**REC-05 — \[MVP\]** User changes page, account, bill or task: invalidate the old proposal. Offer to continue from the new context or return; do not silently force navigation back.

**REC-06 — \[MVP\]** If a local activity/source-reference or optional receipt-copy save fails after payment confirmation, retain the verified result onscreen and offer retrying that local save. State that payment succeeded but local saving did not. Never retry payment to fix storage.

**REC-07 — \[MVP\]** Cancellation is distinct from Close. On “stop this task,” stop future actions, explain any irreversible work already completed and close the workflow with its factual status. No cancellation promise can undo a provider submission.

### **Failure contract across every feature**

**Each error has an honest explanation, retained context, a working recovery control and a defined next state. Preserve user agency without a technical error dump.**

**\[MVP\] Onboarding: failed preference save retains session values and offers Retry/Skip. English only; no unsupported language choices. \[LATER\] localized onboarding and advanced preference support.**

**Conversation: mic denied or ASR unavailable offers Type if reasoning works; LLM unavailable offers Retry/manual continuation; TTS unavailable leaves readable captions. A stalled stream cannot execute partial instructions.**

**\[MVP\] Google: cancelled grant stays disconnected, revoked access offers reconnect/manual continuation, missing/multiple bills ask a short question. \[LATER\] provider selection and browser-email recovery.**

**\[LATER\] Vault/profile failures: unlock/cancel, missing or multiple matches, failed save, expired methods and conflicting suggestion popups must be designed when those features are scheduled. \[MVP\] manual entry and minimal task/Google context are sufficient.**

**Guidance/browser: target missing or changed triggers re-observation; low confidence removes stale label and offers manual help. Dismissing a label is not a task error or a request to pause.**

**Payment: mismatch, invalid code, fee/date changes and unknown submission result take their named branches in section 8\. Payment success plus local save failure stays payment success.**

**\[LATER\] Rich recall/shortcut failures: unavailable source or failed save/open needs retry/edit/remove without false success. \[MVP\] show the current outcome/source and preserve a minimal attempt record; no recall or shortcut product required.**

**Connectivity: show local error/status and keep Pause/Close usable while offline. Slow cloud response shows bounded progress and a cancel/retry path; recover each service independently. Test each boundary through failure inje**ction.

## **13\. Privacy, reliability and validation**

**SEC-01 — \[MVP\]** Encrypt sensitive data in transit and at rest; isolate per user/account. Decide device unlock, unattended access, key management, backup and lost-device behavior before saving real credentials or personal information. ### **Comprehensive device traces for pilot engineering**

**TRACE-01 — \[PILOT\]** The designated developer must have access to complete end-to-end device traces for enrolled pilot devices, covering successful, failed and abandoned tasks, manual browser activity and task switching—not only aggregate events, crashes or user-submitted reports. Record a correlated timeline of app/OS lifecycle, navigation, task/turn states, touch/key action metadata, guidance and UI rendering, relevant DOM/state snapshots, redacted screen recording or equivalent ordered visual frames, tool calls/results, model requests/responses, ASR transcript/endpoint timing, TTS timing/playback/interruption, connector requests/results, network latency/errors, storage errors and crashes. Include diagnostic audio from active voice sessions when covered by enrollment; always-on device operation is not permission to record unrelated ambient conversations.

**TRACE-02 — \[PILOT\]** Provide a searchable internal viewer/export by participant/device, session, task and time, with synchronized UI/action/cloud-event evidence so the engineer can reconstruct what the user saw, what Eliza understood, what it tried, and why it failed. Capture manual steps while Eliza is paused to diagnose resume behavior, under the pilot diagnostic setting independently of the assistant's conversation mic. Correlate local traces with server request IDs and service errors; local operational state plus remote diagnostic access must coexist.

**TRACE-03 — \[PILOT\]** Buffer locally during poor connectivity, upload automatically when available and acknowledge durable receipt. Define capture fidelity, rotation/storage limits and retention before launch; do not silently sample away failures or manual-task periods. Mark trace gaps, redacted regions, dropped frames/events and unavailable OS/service fields explicitly. Test clock alignment, ordering, reconnect, restart, storage exhaustion and an inference failure; the engineer must be able to retrieve a coherent trace without asking the senior to reproduce every issue.

**TRACE-04 — \[PILOT\]** Enroll participants with a clear explanation of what engineering can see, record that authorization, and provide a capture pause/withdrawal path with visible data gaps. Do not expose secret keystrokes through an otherwise comprehensive input log. Screen, conversation and model traces may contain other personal task information, so they remain restricted to authorized pilot/support staff and never flow into distribution-partner reports.

Acceptance: from one pilot-device session, the engineer can follow a user task across local UI, browser, model, ASR/TTS and connector activity, including a pause with manual progress, a cloud failure and a resumed attempt. Trace completeness/gaps and metric task IDs are visible. Secret-entry tests verify masking without destroying the action timeline.

These are proposed targets, not measured capabilities.

**REL-02 — \[MVP\]** Missing or uncertain evidence must produce “I couldn’t confirm” rather than success. A crash must not lose a known provider confirmation or restart a payment automatically. Persist critical state transitions before continuing.

**QA-01 — \[MVP\]** Test helper state transitions, task authorization, stale targets, duplicate events, password/secret exclusion during manual entry, Google revoke/reconnect, single-task persistence and no-agent-submit. \[LATER\] vault cancellation, multi-account/task isolation and richer record-management tests.

**QA-04 — \[PILOT\]** Usability validation with older adults includes finding help, understanding listening/acting states, correcting a field, stopping, resuming and explaining whether payment was submitted or confirmed. Do not treat a scripted agent demo as user validation.

### **Pilot outcomes: independence and reduced caregiver support**

**PILOT-01 — \[PILOT\]** Primary assisted-completion rate \= unique tasks started with Eliza that reach the intended, verified outcome with the senior and Eliza and no material caregiver/researcher/engineer assistance, divided by all unique tasks started with Eliza in the measured start cohort. Record when the user states a goal and Eliza accepts the task; do not count greetings as tasks. Pauses/resumes/retries retain the same ID. Failed, abandoned and unsupported accepted tasks remain in the denominator. Report pending/unknown outcomes explicitly and use a predefined follow-up cutoff rather than dropping them. Success means the user's goal was met, not that the model said Done.

**PILOT-02 — \[PILOT\]** Also report the requested overall completion rate for tasks started with Eliza, regardless of subsequent assistance, split by final support mode. This reveals cases eventually finished by a caregiver. A task that starts alone and later uses Eliza belongs in an “Eliza joined later” cohort rather than being silently added to the started-with-Eliza denominator. A task started with Eliza but ultimately completed manually without its help is reported separately from successful completion with Eliza.

**PILOT-03 — \[PILOT\]** Support mix across all tracked completed tasks: (A) senior alone, with no material Eliza or human help; (B) senior with Eliza, with no material human help; (C) caregiver-supported, including human guidance or takeover even when Eliza also participated. Each percentage uses all tracked completed tasks as the same denominator. Any material human support takes precedence so caregiver help is not credited to Eliza. Report assistance by a caregiver, researcher or engineer separately within C; ordinary setup assistance is recorded separately from help on the measured task. Unknown support is a visible fourth data-quality category, not assigned to A or B.

**PILOT-04 — \[PILOT\]** Measure reduction in caregiver-supported task share as baseline share minus pilot share in percentage points, alongside counts, total attempted/completed tasks and per-participant results. Also report independent completion (A+B) across all tracked task attempts and unresolved/abandoned rates: fewer caregiver calls is not a success if more tasks simply remain undone. Optional caregiver minutes per task and takeover frequency provide supporting evidence.

**PILOT-05 — \[PILOT\]** Define the everyday digital-task categories included in the pilot before enrollment and keep them comparable across baseline and pilot, beginning with bills and any other genuinely supported helper tasks. Capture tasks completed without Eliza on the device as well as tasks with Eliza. Combine device events with short senior/caregiver task diaries or check-ins for assistance and tasks outside observable device activity. A connected-device trace alone cannot establish who helped or represent every task in a person's life. Record source as observed, participant-reported or adjudicated; report missing coverage rather than claiming an all-task denominator from Eliza-only telemetry.

**PILOT-06 — \[PILOT\]** Use a pre-pilot baseline and follow-up periods with comparable participants, task types and billing opportunities; choose duration, sample size, minimum task counts and target improvement before evaluating results. Report numerator/denominator, participant count, task mix, uncertainty and missing-data rate. Include participant-level paired changes so heavy users do not dominate the result. Without a comparison design supporting causality, describe the measured change during the pilot rather than claiming Eliza alone caused it.

**PILOT-07 — \[PILOT\]** A distribution-partner evidence report must show assisted completion, total completion, the alone/Eliza/caregiver support mix before and during the pilot, caregiver-share change, unresolved work and representative verified examples. Separate product impact from debug/reliability measures. Targets are to be agreed before launch, not invented in this PRD; any partner-facing case example needs appropriate permission and removal of identifying information.

**PILOT-08 — \[PILOT\]** Test metric computation using known fixtures: successful Eliza task, caregiver takeover, researcher rescue, retry/resume, task switching, unsupported request, unknown outcome, independent task, Eliza joined later and missing support data. Review ambiguous completion/support classification against source evidence; never use unverified model self-report alone. Dashboards must expose both counts and rates, with trace links available only to authorized internal reviewers.

Supporting diagnostics remain useful: step completion, recovery rate, code retrieval, latency, interruption rate and time stuck. They explain failures and improvement opportunities; they do not substitute for independence outcomes.

Release gates by priority: \[MVP\] pass the single-task example and focused failure tests, enforce user-submit/secret-exclusion boundaries, and provide developer logs plus task events. \[PILOT\] comprehensive traces, verified independence instrumentation/baseline, enrollment and support readiness. \[LATER\] password manager, extra connectors, Spanish, assistive-technology support, multi-task chooser and shortcuts do not block MVP acceptance.

# Screen Share PRD

## Oct 19, 2026 · @Camilla

## **Scope and principles**

Screen sharing lets a helper see and act on the senior's Grace tablet from their phone. It's mainly for unblocking a task Grace couldn't finish, but either of them can start a session anytime. Sources: Companion App Chat Log (exported 19 Oct 2026\) and Screen Share Prototype. Reference files: Unblock Flow, Shared Control Map, Mirror Options (E), Dock, Grace Tablet Prototype. This doc covers behavior and rules; visual design and exact copy live in the Screen Share Prototype. The tablet follows the standard Grace panel and dock designs.

The helper's companion app (Home, Needs you, task pages, Calendar, Grace chat, Settings and its visual rules) has its own doc, [Companion App Requirements](https://claude.ai/artifact/GY2MWuNLvt99U89o1EFYfP). This doc keeps only the parts of it that start, shape or follow a screen-sharing call.

* **Senior:** the older adult using the Grace tablet. It's their screen and their session.  
* **Helper:** someone listed under People & access, usually an adult child, using the companion app on their phone.  
* **Grace:** the AI assistant that works through tasks on the tablet.

Principles:

* **The senior is in charge.** Nothing happens on their tablet without their say. Grace asks before she acts or brings in the helper, silence counts as no, and nothing ends or hands over on its own. The senior or the helper can stop Grace at any time.  
* For example: no answer counts as a decline (R4); postponing never escalates (S4).  
* **Familiar over novel.** Borrow patterns people already know, so no one learns something new under stress.  
* For example: the request reads as a call (R3); the helper types on their phone's standard keyboard (K1).  
* **One shared reality.** Both people see the same screen and always know who did what.  
* For example: every action carries the actor's name (A1); changes of control are announced (C3).  
* **Warn, don't block.** Trust the helper. Show who did what instead of adding extra approval steps.  
* For example: the helper can pay without a second approval, because the senior was told up front (P2, R5).  
* **Show, don't tell.** Prefer on-screen cues, like name labels and outlines, over written notices.  
* For example: name labels show who's acting (A4); a short notice announces each change of control (C3).  
* **One thing at a time.** Each screen has one focus, with nothing competing for attention.  
* For example: the joining state shows one card and nothing else (J1); only one keyboard shows at a time (K1).  
* **Looking is free.** Exploring never changes anything. Only a deliberate tap or typing does.  
* For example: scrolling or zooming never changes control (C4); a long-press points without taking control (C6).  
* **Control follows the hands.** Whoever touches the screen is in control, with no modes or buttons to manage.  
* For example: whoever tapped last has control (C1); there's no keyboard button (K2).  
* **People talk, Grace steps back.** When two people are in a session, Grace makes room for them.  
* For example: Grace stays silent and writes instead (G3); her chat closes when sharing stops, so a call is just a call (G7).  
* **One pattern per job.** The same kind of moment looks the same everywhere, on both devices.  
* For example: attribution works the same in both directions (A3); the call controls are the same on both devices (V1).  
* **Simple, clear, concise copy.** Say the least that does the job, in plain words.  
* For example: the request tells the senior one thing (R5); Grace's pre-call summary is a short paragraph (S8).  
* **Just in time.** Give information at the moment it's needed, not up front.  
* For example: Stop sharing appears once sharing starts, not in the request (R5); step-by-step detail shows in Grace's chat once the call starts (S7).

## **Out of MVP**

* Video while the screen is shared. Video is available once sharing stops (V1).  
* Double approval for payments.  
* Grace speaking aloud during a share.  
* A detailed, action-by-action audit log of a share. MVP records key events only (A6).  
* Recovery after a dropped call, like reconnecting automatically. In MVP, a dropped call ends the session and the task pauses (E7).  
* Pointing by the senior. In MVP only the helper can point (C6, A7).  
* Richer pre-call context (S8): when the senior plans to come back to a task, whether another helper is already on it, and a separate "have ready" list.  
* Starting a screen-share call from the tablet's contacts. In MVP, the senior calls through Grace or from Tasks (S5).

# Companion App Requirements

## Oct 21, 2026 · @Camilla

## **Scope**

The companion app is the phone app for the people who help a Grace user (here, Anna helping her mum, Margaret). It answers three questions: is Mom okay, does anything need me, and what's coming up. It's also where the helper starts a call.

The call itself (ringing, joining, the shared screen, control, Grace during the call and ending) is covered in Screen Share Requirements. This doc covers everything around it. Requirement IDs here start with C so they don't clash with the S, J, V and G IDs there.

The working prototype is Helper Phone Layouts.

| C2 | Home is where the helper lands. Top to bottom: a large Home title (every tab root has one, as in iOS, so content never starts right under the status bar), the senior's status card, Needs you, and Later today (the next three events, leaving out daily reminders, each with a one-line detail, each opening its details, with Calendar ›). ## **Open questions**

* **Who can change someone's access?** Decided: the senior always can, on her tablet; a helper can only with Manage access, which only she can give (C20). Still open: whether a helper with Manage access needs the senior to approve each change on her tablet, as she does for invites.  
* **What if a helper looks after more than one person** (Mom and Dad)? The app assumes one senior. Options: a switcher on the status card, or one Home that combines Needs you across both.  
* **Can Mom see the Activity log?** Proposal: yes, on her tablet. If everything in it is something she'd be fine with her kids seeing, that's also the log's privacy test.  
* **How long do money and safety entries stay?** Activity shows 7 days. An audit trail that disappears after a week is weak; money, safety and access entries may need to be kept longer and searchable.  
* **Can an event be hidden from someone?** Everyone with calendar access sees every event; there's no per-event sharing. Open: whether Mom can keep one event from one person (an appointment she doesn't want a sibling to see), and whether that's a per-event switch or part of access in People & access. Rare, but it matters when it happens.

## **Later: medication follow-through**

Out of scope for the pilot. Pill reminders stay ordinary daily calendar reminders (C28): Grace reminds Mom on her tablet at the set time, helpers see the reminder on the calendar, and nothing records whether she took them. Grace doesn't alert a helper about a missed dose.

Why later: confirming medication is a safety promise. It needs a reliable way for the senior to confirm, a rule for who is alerted and when, and care about what a missed dose means for each medication. Half of it is worse than none, because helpers would start relying on it.

What was designed, to pick up later (shown in prototype versions before 138):

* **Needs you row.** A missed dose uses the task row: "Lunchtime pills" / "Needs a call from you", with a red "3 hours late" tag. It opens a page whose main action is Call Mom.  
* **Reminders carry the outcome.** Calendar row: "Every day · Taken" in green, "Not taken" in amber. The event page leads with a status line ("Taken · 8:04 AM", "Not taken · Grace reminded her twice", "Grace reminds her at 6 PM"), and when the dose is in Needs you, an In Needs you row opens that task. Past days show Taken or Not taken, future days nothing. Home's detail line shows the result.  
* **Call ended.** After a pills call the page asks "Did she take her lunchtime pills?" with She took them / Not yet. She took them marks it done ("Taken · you checked on a call"); Not yet keeps it in Needs you with a "Called 4:10 PM" tag and a line in its history, and Grace reminds her again.  
* **Notification.** Missed pills (Time Sensitive): "Mom hasn't taken her lunchtime pills" / "Grace reminded her at 1 PM and 1:30 PM. A call from you usually does it." → the pills task. The evening summary would include pills taken.  
* **Activity.** Pill entries ("Mom took her morning pills", "7:40 PM · evening pills were late") and a Pills filter.

Open questions for that phase:

* **Who gets the missed-pills alert?** C10 sends a help notification only to the person the senior approved. For missed pills she didn't ask anyone. Options: everyone with Needs you alerts on, with "Anna is calling" shown to the others; or one person at a time, in an order she sets. Without a rule, two helpers can call at once.  
* **When does a missed dose become Needs you?** Proposal: Grace reminds at the dose time and again 30 minutes later; if there's no answer 60 minutes after the dose, it moves to Needs you. Open: whether the senior can snooze a dose, and whether the timing varies by medication.  
* **How does the senior confirm?** Tapping Done on the reminder, telling Grace, or both, and what counts when she doesn't answer.

## **Later: emotional check-ins**

Out of scope for the pilot. In the pilot Grace reports facts to helpers: what's planned, what happened, what's paused, and what Mom told her about a task. She doesn't judge or report how Mom seems or feels (mood, loneliness, confusion, "she seemed down today"). That kind of emotional reasoning is easy to get wrong, can worry a family for no reason or miss something real, and touches what Mom would want shared. So there's no "How is Mom today?" suggestion, the Grace tab's opening line and the Evening summary stick to events, and the summary is described as "What happened with Mom today". Open for that phase: what signals Grace would use, how confident she must be before saying anything, whether Mom sees and approves what's shared, and when a concern becomes a Needs you item rather than a remark.

Settled: Activity opens from a clock icon in the Grace tab (C15); video is in MVP, but never at the same time as screen sharing (Screen Share Requirements V1); the earlier Calendar · Activity · Grace · Mom structure is retired in favor of C1.

# **What Phase 1 must prove**

On the custom Android fork, the user can ask Grace for help paying their Chase credit-card bill and complete the payment on the real website. Grace prepares the task and provides voice assistance plus readable on-screen guidance; the user controls approval, corrections and personal information.

This is a live working prototype of one supported journey toward broader assistance. Chase is the only required website; other sites are exploratory. A recording can help circulation but is not a fallback.

# **Approved experience**

## **Start and sign in**

Grace clarifies the request when needed, opens the browser, identifies the correct Chase website and guides private sign-in. The user enters passwords and verification codes privately. Phase 1 does not store them; password-manager integration is deferred until after Phase 1\.

## **Prepare and approve payment**

Grace reads current account information and fills payment details from spoken or typed choices, page evidence and approved memory. The user can tap the website’s payment button or explicitly authorize Grace to submit by voice.

Before Grace submits, she shows and reads back the amount, payment date and funding account, then waits for explicit approval of those details. Corrections invalidate approval: “Yes, but pay the minimum on Friday” updates the proposal and requires fresh confirmation. An early or unrelated yes never submits the old plan. Exact dialogue is flexible.

## **Verify the result**

Grace checks the actual website result and reports confirmed, pending, verified failure or uncertain status.

Confirmed success: explain the verified outcome.

Verified failure: explain the issue, correct it where supported and offer another attempt with fresh approval.

Pending or uncertain: investigate the existing attempt before another submission. A missing confirmation screen alone does not prove failure.

## **Guide without overwhelming**

Show one clear next step at a time alongside the website, updating as the user progresses, including private sign-in and payment approval. The user can interrupt, ask questions, request repetition or change details. Grace pauses conflicting actions and understands the complete request before continuing.

## **Talk, type and recover**

Talk and Type stay available in the same conversation. Switching preserves context and the existing draft. Type stops listening; typed exchanges keep the mic off until Talk.

After unusable speech, Grace asks for repetition once. After a second consecutive miss, she stops listening and offers the existing Talk and Type options without guessing or losing progress. Silence and ambiguous information have separate handling.

## **Close and continue**

Closing the browser stops website activity and goes to the Home screen; the conversation persists unchanged and is there when Grace is next opened. After payment, preserve the verified status. Before completion, pause the task; asking to resume reopens the website and checks its current state before continuing.

Closing Grace hides the conversation and pauses work. Reopening restores the conversation; work resumes only when requested.

## **Remember with permission**

Retain useful personal details across sessions with approval. Support review, correction and deletion by voice or touch. Passwords and verification codes are excluded from Phase 1 memory.
